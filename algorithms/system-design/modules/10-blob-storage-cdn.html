Blob storage and CDNs: big files, stored cheaply, served from nearby

Photos, videos, documents, backups and model weights are too big and too numerous for a database. They go in object storage, which is cheap, nearly impossible to lose data from, and effectively unlimited. Users then download them through a CDN, a network of servers close to them. The design skill is keeping your servers out of the way: clients upload straight to storage, and downloads come from the edge.

1. The idea

A furniture shop doesn't carry sofas through its showroom. Customers order at the counter (your API), the sofa goes straight from the factory to a warehouse, and delivery comes from the depot nearest the customer. The counter only handles paperwork. Make your servers the counter: they approve and record; the heavy goods travel another way.
  • Object storage (Amazon S3, Google Cloud Storage, Azure Blob Storage, and S3-compatible services such as Cloudflare R2) stores objects: a blob of bytes plus a little metadata, under a key like uploads/u42/2026/10/7f3a.jpg, inside a bucket. There are no folders, no partial updates and no queries: you PUT, GET, DELETE and LIST whole objects.
  • Durability comes from copies or erasure-coded pieces spread across devices and data centres. S3 is designed for 99.999999999% (eleven nines) durability, storing data across at least three availability zones by default.
  • The database keeps the metadata: who owns the file, its key, size, type, status, and anything you'll query. Files themselves never go in the database.
  • A CDN (CloudFront, Cloudflare, Akamai, Fastly, Google Cloud CDN) caches copies at hundreds of edge locations. A user in Sydney gets the image from Sydney, not from Virginia.

Rule of thumb: anything over about a megabyte that you won't query inside, and certainly anything over 10 MB, belongs in object storage.

2. How it works

Uploading a video, end to end

Press Play to follow a 2 GB video from a phone to viewers around the world, without its bytes ever passing through your app servers.

The pieces in that flow

  • Presigned URL: your server signs a URL that grants one action (PUT this key) for a short time, with limits such as size and content type. Signing happens locally with your credentials, no call to storage needed. S3 presigned URLs can be valid for up to 7 days with long-term credentials; in practice use minutes. Google calls them signed URLs and Azure uses SAS tokens.
  • Multipart upload: big files are sent in parts (S3: 5 MiB to 5 GiB per part, up to 10,000 parts, objects up to 48.8 TiB). Parts upload in parallel and can be retried individually; a failed upload resumes from the missing part instead of restarting. A lifecycle rule should delete abandoned uploads, which otherwise cost money silently.
  • Events: storage announces new objects (S3 can notify SNS, SQS, Lambda or EventBridge). That event, not the client saying "done", marks the upload complete and triggers processing. A periodic reconciliation job catches anything an event missed.
  • Consistency: S3 gives strong read-after-write consistency for PUTs and DELETEs: once a write succeeds, every read sees it.

How a CDN serves a file

  1. The user requests https://cdn.example.com/v/7f3a/720p/seg42.ts. DNS or anycast routes them to the nearest edge.
  2. Edge hit: the file is cached there; it's returned in a few milliseconds.
  3. Edge miss: the edge asks a regional cache or an origin shield (one designated cache layer in front of your origin), and only if that misses, the origin (your bucket or servers).
  4. How long it may be cached is set by the Cache-Control header from the origin (try the builder in section 3).
  5. To change a cached file: give the new version a new URL (app.3f9c2.js, best), or purge it through the CDN's API (slower, sometimes limited).
  6. Private content: the CDN checks a signed URL or signed cookie at the edge, so you can protect paid videos without every request reaching your servers.

Video streaming works this way too: the video is cut into short segments in several qualities, listed in a playlist (HLS or DASH). The player downloads segments over plain HTTP and switches quality as bandwidth changes, so CDNs cache video like any other file.

    4. Trade-offs

    Direct upload vs through your servers

    Direct (presigned) uploads are faster, cheaper and scale without you. Proxying through your servers only makes sense when every byte must be checked before storage (some compliance scanning), and even then, stream in chunks.

    Long TTL vs freshness

    Long TTLs maximise hits; changes then need new URLs or purges. Version file names for static assets, and use short TTLs (or stale-while-revalidate) for things like a public API response.

    Storage classes

    Frequently read data in the standard class; rarely read data in infrequent-access or archive classes that cost much less to store but more (and slower) to read. Lifecycle rules move objects automatically as they age.

    Egress is the hidden cost

    Storing a terabyte is cheap; sending it to the internet can cost far more. CDNs usually lower transfer prices and offload the origin. Check each provider's current pricing pages: these numbers change.

    Object storage (S3 and equivalents)

    The default for files · bytes here, metadata in the DB

    Effectively unlimited storage for whole objects (bytes plus a little metadata) under keys in buckets, designed for 11 nines of durability.

    How it works

    PUT, GET, DELETE and LIST whole objects; no partial updates and no queries. S3 gives strong read-after-write consistency. The rich metadata (owner, status, size, permissions) belongs in your database, not in object tags: S3 allows only 10 tags per object and you can't query them.

    Status today (checked October 2026)

    Amazon S3, Google Cloud Storage, Azure Blob Storage, and S3-compatible services (Cloudflare R2, MinIO). The standard home for media, documents, backups and model weights.

    Use it when

    • Any file over about 10 MB, and any file you'd never query with SQL
    • Media, documents, backups, logs, data-lake files

    Skip it when

    • Small values that are part of a record (a 2 KB JSON blob can live in the database)
    • Data that needs partial updates or queries

    In the interview

    The moment files appear: Metadata in the database, bytes in S3.

    🚩 Red flags
    • Storing images or videos as blobs in the database.
    • Rich metadata only in object tags, which you can't query.

    Presigned URLs

    The default for uploads and private downloads

    A URL your server signs, granting one action (PUT or GET) on one key for a short time, so clients talk to storage directly.

    How it works

    • The server checks the user and their quota, picks the key (uploads/{user}/{date}/{uuid}, never the client's file name), and signs the URL locally: no call to storage.
    • Sign in limits: content length range and content type, so the URL can't be used to upload a 50 GB file. Anyone holding the URL can use it until it expires (minutes, not days).
    • At the same moment, create the database row with status = 'pending' and the key.

    Status today

    Presigned URLs on S3, signed URLs on Google Cloud Storage, SAS tokens on Azure.

    Use it when

    • Any upload or download larger than a normal API payload
    • Private files that shouldn't be public

    Skip it when

    • Uploads that must be validated synchronously before accepting (a CSV whose header must be right)
    • Compliance scanning before storage: proxy those, streaming in chunks

    In the interview

    The API returns a presigned PUT URL valid for 15 minutes with a size limit; the client uploads straight to S3.

    🚩 Red flags
    • Proxying uploads through app servers without a reason: they become slow, expensive pipes.
    • Client-chosen keys, or URLs without size limits.
    • Trusting a client "upload done" call to mark the file ready: races, fake completions, orphaned files. Use the storage event plus a reconciliation job.

    Multipart and resumable uploads

    Situational · big files, flaky networks

    Split a big upload into parts that go in parallel and can be retried individually, then stitch them together.

    How it works

    S3 multipart: parts of at least 5 MiB, each with its own presigned URL, up to 10,000 parts. On failure, list the parts already uploaded and send only the missing ones; finish with a complete call carrying the part list and checksums. Progress = parts done. A lifecycle rule deletes abandoned uploads after a day or two, because unfinished parts cost money. Google Cloud Storage and Azure use a single session URL with chunks instead.

    Status today

    Standard on every object store; most SDKs do it automatically above a size threshold.

    Use it when

    • Files larger than about 100 MB
    • Mobile uploads that may be interrupted

    Skip it when

    • Small files: one PUT is simpler

    In the interview

    Videos upload in 10 MB parts; if the phone loses signal at 60%, the app lists the finished parts and resumes from part 4.

    🚩 Red flags
    • Restarting a 2 GB upload from zero after a failure at 99%.
    • No lifecycle rule for abandoned uploads.

    CDN delivery with signed URLs

    The default for downloads at scale

    Serve files from CDN edges near users; protect private ones with signed URLs or signed cookies the edge verifies itself.

    How it works

    The CDN checks the signature with a public key at the edge, so private videos are protected without every request reaching your servers. Popular files go from roughly 200 ms to a few milliseconds after the first request, and the CDN absorbs most traffic. HTTP range requests let downloads resume and players seek.

    Status today

    CloudFront signed URLs and cookies, Google Cloud CDN and Media CDN, Azure Front Door, Cloudflare and Fastly.

    Use it when

    • Media and downloads at any real scale
    • Paid or private content (signed URLs stop hotlinking)

    Skip it when

    • Rarely accessed private files, where a presigned storage URL is simpler and cheaper

    In the interview

    Downloads go through CloudFront with signed URLs that expire in an hour.

    🚩 Red flags
    • Serving large files from app servers, or straight from one region's bucket to the whole world.

    Storage classes and lifecycle rules

    Situational · cost at scale

    Cheaper tiers for data that's rarely read, and rules that move or delete objects as they age.

    How it works

    Standard for hot data; infrequent-access and archive classes cost far less to store but more, and more slowly, to read. Lifecycle rules move objects between classes after N days and delete expired ones (including abandoned multipart uploads).

    Status today

    Every provider offers several classes and lifecycle policies; check current pricing pages, which change.

    Use it when

    • Petabytes of media where most items are rarely watched after a month
    • Backups and compliance archives

    Skip it when

    • Small datasets: the savings don't matter

    In the interview

    Originals move to an infrequent-access class after 30 days and to archive after a year.

    🚩 Red flags
    • Ignoring egress cost: sending data out often costs far more than storing it.

    How a CDN works inside

    Situational · CDN-heavy designs

    The choices behind a CDN: who puts content at the edge, how many cache tiers, what can be cached, and how edges stay fresh.

    How it works

    • Pull vs push: a pull CDN fetches from your origin on the first miss (the default for websites and most media); a push CDN is loaded ahead of time (large files, scheduled releases, video before a premiere).
    • Request routing: anycast or DNS sends each user to a nearby, healthy edge.
    • Tiers: edges ask regional caches, which ask an origin shield, which asks the origin, so a popular file is fetched from the origin once rather than by every edge.
    • Dynamic content: personalised pages can still use the CDN's network (TLS near the user, connection reuse to the origin), and edge compute (Workers, Lambda@Edge) can assemble or authorise responses at the edge.
    • Freshness: versioned URLs for static assets (best), short TTLs with stale-while-revalidate for things that change, purge APIs for urgent removals.

    Status today

    Cloudflare, Akamai, Fastly, CloudFront and the clouds' own CDNs all offer tiered caching, shields, signed URLs and edge compute; push-style prefetch exists for big releases.

    Use it when

    • Global audiences, media, software downloads, launches with predictable spikes (push or prefetch)

    Skip it when

    • Personal data cached under shared keys

    In the interview

    A pull CDN with an origin shield; assets have versioned URLs and year-long TTLs, and the API sends short TTLs with stale-while-revalidate.

    🚩 Red flags
    • Purging the CDN on every change instead of versioning URLs.
    • Assuming a CDN only helps static files: it also terminates TLS near users and keeps warm connections to the origin.

    Inside a blob store

    Situational · "design S3" questions

    How a service like S3 stores trillions of objects durably: a metadata service, chunk servers, and replication or erasure coding.

    How it works

    • Front end: authenticates requests and routes them.
    • Metadata service: maps bucket + key to the object's chunks and their locations; it's a sharded, replicated database, and the part that must be strongly consistent.
    • Data (chunk) servers: store fixed-size chunks on disk, spread across racks and zones.
    • Durability: three full replicas cost 3× the space. Erasure coding splits an object into, say, 6 data pieces plus 3 parity pieces: any 6 of the 9 rebuild it, for 1.5× the space. Replicas suit hot, small data; erasure coding suits large, colder data.
    • Background work: scrubbing (re-reading data to catch silent corruption with checksums), repairing lost pieces, and garbage-collecting deleted objects.

    Status today

    S3, Google Cloud Storage and Azure Blob Storage use variants of this; open-source equivalents include Ceph and MinIO. S3 is designed for 11 nines of durability.

    Use it when

    • Infrastructure interviews: "design a blob store", "design Dropbox's storage layer"

    Skip it when

    • Product designs: there you use S3, you don't build it

    In the interview

    Metadata in a sharded, replicated store; data as erasure-coded chunks across three zones, so losing a whole zone loses nothing.

    🚩 Red flags
    • Storing three full copies of petabytes without considering erasure coding's cost savings.
    • Forgetting scrubbing: disks corrupt data silently; checksums catch it.

    6. Remember it

    Memory hook: "Servers sign, storage carries, edges deliver"Your servers only sign permissions and record metadata; the bytes go straight to object storage; downloads come from the CDN edge nearest the user.
    How this hook works: what "Servers sign, storage carries, edges deliver" means

    What the slogan means, phrase by phrase

    "Servers sign"
    The API checks the user and quota, creates a pending metadata row, and returns a presigned URL. It never touches the file's bytes.
    "storage carries"
    The client uploads directly (multipart for big files); the storage service holds the bytes durably and announces the upload with an event.
    "edges deliver"
    Readers download through the CDN, which caches near them and checks signatures for private content.

    Why this picture fits: it's the furniture shop again: the counter does paperwork, the warehouse holds stock, the local depot delivers. Nobody carries sofas through the showroom.

    The story → the concept

    The delivery note for one item, valid todayA presigned URL: one key, one action, short expiry

    Watch it on an example

    1. A user shares a 40 MB photo album in a chat app.
    2. Servers sign: the API returns 8 presigned URLs and creates 8 pending rows.
    3. Storage carries: the phone uploads in parallel; storage events flip each row to "ready" and trigger thumbnails.
    4. Edges deliver: 500 group members open the album; the CDN serves the thumbnails from their nearest edge, and the origin serves each file roughly once per region.

    The trap it saves you from

    Uploading through app servers. They become slow, expensive pipes that tie up memory and connections for minutes per file, and they're the first thing to fall over.

    It still works when…

    The "files" are machine learning models or dataset shards: store them as objects, give jobs temporary credentials, and let many readers pull from storage or a cache layer instead of a single server (Module 29).

    7. Common mistakes

    Trusting the client's "upload finished" callClients crash, lie or lose connections. Mark uploads complete from storage events, and reconcile pending rows periodically.
    Storing files in the databaseHuge rows bloat backups, slow replication and waste expensive storage. Store the key, not the bytes.
    Presigned URLs without limitsAn unlimited, long-lived upload URL is an open bucket. Sign a size limit and content type, and keep expiry short.
    Caching private files publiclyA CDN that caches /invoice/123.pdf without checking access can serve it to anyone. Use private or signed URLs.
    Overwriting files at the same URLEdges and browsers keep the old one until it expires. Put a version or content hash in the name.

    8. Design exercise (25 minutes)

    1. Design profile photos for an app with 50M users: the upload API, the bucket key scheme, thumbnail generation, the metadata row and its statuses, and how the app shows the photo.
    2. Make it resumable: users on trains upload 500 MB videos. Describe what the client stores, what happens at 80% when the connection drops, and how abandoned uploads are cleaned up.
    3. Choose headers with the builder for: your JavaScript bundle, a user's private invoice PDF, and a public "trending" API response that may be 30 seconds old.
    4. Size the CDN: 100,000 requests a second for 10,000 popular images across 300 edges, TTL one day. Roughly how many requests reach the origin? With a shield?
    5. Write it down: the upload flow in five steps, in your design notebook.

    9. Self-check quiz

    With a presigned URL, who sends the file's bytes to storage?

    The server only signs a short-lived permission; the bytes go straight from the client to object storage.

    What should mark an upload as complete in your database?

    The storage service knows the object really exists. Back it up with a reconciliation job for missed events.

    You ship a new version of app.js, cached for a year at the CDN. Best way to make users get it?

    Versioned names (app.3f9c2.js) are fresh by definition, and the old ones can stay cached safely.

    A multipart upload fails at 90%. What happens?

    Parts are stored independently; the client uploads only the missing ones and then completes the upload.

    What should mark an uploaded file as ready in your database?

    Client "done" calls can be faked, lost or early. The storage event carries the key; reconciliation catches lost events.

    A 2 GB upload fails at 99%. What's the right design?

    Parts are retried individually; the client keeps the upload ID and sends only the missing parts.

    Why do large blob stores use erasure coding rather than three full copies for cold data?

    6 + 3 coding stores 1.5× the data and survives any 3 lost pieces; three replicas store 3×. Reads and repairs cost more CPU and network.
